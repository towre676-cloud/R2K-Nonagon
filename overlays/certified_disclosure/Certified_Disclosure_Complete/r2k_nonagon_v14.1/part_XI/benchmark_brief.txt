The **most impressive external benchmarks for R2K Nonagon are not generic inverse-problem datasets**. They are benchmarks where its new elimination calculus has a chance to do something unusually strong: **recover hidden physical structure from sparse interventions, quantify exactly what is identifiable, localize changes, and attach certificates to the answer.** Based on the current v10/v10.1 machinery, I would rank the targets like this. 

1. **IEEE/MATPOWER power-grid cases — especially IEEE 118, IEEE 300, then the 1,354–3,000+ bus PEGASE/Polish cases.** This is probably the single most impressive computational benchmark family we can attack. MATPOWER provides internationally recognizable network cases ranging from IEEE 14/30/57/118/300 through much larger 1,354-, 2,383-, 2,869-, 3,012-bus systems. [MATPOWER](https://matpower.org/docs/ref/matpower5.0/case118.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) The clean Nonagon experiment is not “solve AC power flow better than MATPOWER”; that would miss our strength. Instead: hide the branch graph and parameters, expose only a small set of port responses, permit controlled shunt/impedance perturbations, and ask Nonagon to recover topology and weights. Then progressively introduce finite-load error, sensor noise, inaccessible buses, line outages and parameter drift. The killer headline would be something like **“118-node or 300-node externally defined network recovered from local response interventions, with explicit identifiability and resolution certificates.”** If the calculus scales into the PEGASE/Polish networks, that becomes spectacular.

2. **Z24 Bridge progressive-damage benchmark.** For a *real physical measured system*, this may be even more persuasive than IEEE 118. Z24 is a genuine full-scale bridge benchmark, monitored for nearly a year and then deliberately damaged before demolition. KU Leuven explicitly describes it as benchmark data for system identification, modal analysis, environmental normalization and vibration-based damage identification. [Civil Engineering KU Leuven](https://bwk.kuleuven.be/bwm/z24?utm_source=chatgpt.com) This lets us translate the electrical mathematics through the mechanical dynamic-stiffness analogue and ask a much more interesting question than standard damage classification: **can Nonagon infer where the effective stiffness/damping network changed, how much it changed, and whether the data actually resolve that conclusion?** A convincing localization of controlled Z24 damage from restricted sensors would be a very serious external result.

3. **IASC–ASCE Structural Health Monitoring Benchmark.** This is almost tailor-made for the epistemic part of Nonagon because the benchmark was explicitly designed to permit comparison among SHM methods and includes the nasty ingredients we care about: **measurement noise, modeling error, missing excitation measurements and limited sensors**. [ASCE Library](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/abs/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-9399%282004%29130%3A1%283%29?utm_source=chatgpt.com) We should turn its damage cases into an intervention/reconstruction campaign and report topology/stiffness recovery alongside conventional damage-location/severity metrics. Unlike a black-box classifier, Nonagon could potentially say *why* an edge/change is resolvable or non-resolvable.

4. **SINTEF Vector Fitting measured frequency-response examples.** This is the benchmark I would use to challenge the **spectral half of R2K directly**. Vector Fitting is one of the standard engineering methods for extracting rational pole/residue models from measured or computed frequency responses; SINTEF notes its use in power systems, microwave systems, high-speed electronics and electronic packages, and now supplies matrix/passive fitting machinery as well. [SINTEF](https://www.sintef.no/en/software/vector-fitting/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) We should run the exact same responses through **Vector Fitting vs Nonagon** and score held-out frequency error, pole recovery, passivity violations, model order, conditioning under noise, runtime—and, crucially, whether Nonagon can proceed from the fitted response into **physical-network disclosure and certified zero/pole statements**. That last part is where the comparison becomes much more interesting than “our rational fit is 2% smaller.”

5. **CALCE measured lithium-ion impedance data.** CALCE publicly provides experimental battery data including **impedance measurements**, multiple chemistries and several cell formats. [calce.umd.edu](https://calce.umd.edu/battery-data?utm_source=chatgpt.com) We already learned from our first measured-cell work that a naive RC interpretation is too restrictive; that makes CALCE a *better* benchmark rather than a worse one. We should compare RC, RL/RLC or generalized passive realizations against established equivalent-circuit identification, with held-out frequencies and cells. The high-value result would be demonstrating that Nonagon can automatically say **“this topology is impossible,” “this extra dynamic branch is demanded,” or “these two candidate circuits cannot be distinguished at the present resolution.”** That would showcase elimination calculus rather than merely curve fitting. 

6. **LANL three-story structural benchmark.** The Los Alamos three-story building is an actual experimentally measured structure and remains a recognized SHM test case; recent work still uses its measured vibration data specifically as an experimental benchmark. [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/stc/9210579?utm_source=chatgpt.com) It is smaller than Z24, which is actually useful: we can attempt **near-complete inverse reconstruction**, not merely damage classification. This could be the first external measured-data benchmark where we expose every internal mathematical stage—spectral extraction → dynamic matrix → elimination identities → damage localization → certificate.

7. **DaISy system-identification database.** DaISy exists explicitly so different identification methods can be run on common external datasets, with reproducibility encouraged by the maintainers. [homes.esat.kuleuven.be](https://homes.esat.kuleuven.be/~smc/daisy/daisydata.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) It is less glamorous than Z24 or IEEE 300, but scientifically it is excellent because we could run a **broad battery rather than one cherry-picked case**. I would use it as the “breadth” experiment: dozens of systems, fixed protocol, conventional system-ID baselines, held-out prediction, noise sensitivity and automatic model-order determination.

The crucial distinction is that I would **not make zeta zeros, Sinai billiards, or another synthetic mass–spring family our headline external benchmark anymore**. Those remain excellent mathematical stress tests, but v10.1 has moved Nonagon into territory where a reader can reasonably ask: *does this recover something somebody else actually measured or standardized?* We can now answer that with bridge structures, power networks, RF responses and batteries. 

**If I were choosing only three for the publication-grade campaign, I would choose:**

| Priority | Benchmark | What makes the result impressive |
|---|---|---|
| **#1** | **IEEE 118 → IEEE 300 → PEGASE/Polish grids** | Scale + exact known topology + controlled hiding/intervention experiment |
| **#2** | **Z24 Bridge** | Full-scale physical structure + real measured progressive damage |
| **#3** | **SINTEF Vector Fitting cases** | Direct confrontation with a major established frequency-domain identification technique |

And I would use **IASC–ASCE + LANL + CALCE + DaISy** as the broader validation suite.

The really ambitious benchmark table should therefore not just say “accuracy.” For every target, I want us reporting **unknown scalar parameters recovered / measurements used / intervention count / held-out response error / topology precision-recall / parameter error / fault-localization accuracy / minimum resolvable perturbation / runtime / certificate success / baseline result**. Nonagon's most unusual metric should be **measurement efficiency**: how many independent readings it needs to disclose a system relative to the number of hidden degrees of freedom. With the current \(n(n+2)\)-reading construction and the frequency-for-pairs tradeoff, that is exactly the sort of graph that could make the work memorable. 

If **IEEE 118/300 topology disclosure + Z24 real-damage localization + Vector Fitting head-to-head** all work, I would consider that a much stronger external validation package than anything currently in the Nonagon archive. It would test **scale, reality, and incumbent competition** separately—and the three together would make a very compelling case that the elimination calculus is a genuine engineering methodology rather than an internally successful mathematical construction.
