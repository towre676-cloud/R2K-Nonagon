The Rank-Two Nonagon XIV

From record to instrument: the noise floor of one port, an estimator that reaches it, how many ports a grid needs, why actuation and not mathematics is the bottleneck, and strong interventions without passivity
C · Independent research · New Britain, Connecticut · 31 October 2026

Abstract. Part XIII recovered every branch of a 70,000-bus grid exactly from one port. Measured against conventional practice, that result left four obstacles to use, and Part XIV takes each one up with a computation. The noise floor. The Cramér–Rao bound for the one-port known-support experiment is computed in binary64 from its analytic Jacobian. To bring the median branch Cramér–Rao standard deviation to 1%, the reading noise must be at most of the port compliance, and the full $n(n+1)/2$ protocol buys only a factor of 2 to 3 for 16 to 41 times the readings. One port is therefore poorly conditioned under the stated local unbiased Gaussian model; changing the estimator does not remove that Cramér–Rao limitation. An efficient estimator. Levenberg–Marquardt on the raw readings, started from the Part XII linear estimate or from a nominal model 10% off, reaches the bound. The median ratio of error to the Cramér–Rao standard deviation is , against 0.674 for an exactly efficient Gaussian estimator, and the Part XII estimator's error falls by a factor of about 14. Ports. With $k$ measuring ports and one weak intervention per bus, the floor falls steeply. On IEEE 118 with 20 ports, readings at $10^{-3}$ noise give a median branch error of with the efficient estimator. That is comparable to phasor-measurement line-parameter estimation, which reports 0.3–0.8% on 10-branch subnetworks with sensors at both ends of every line. On the larger grids the same 20 ports need about ten times less noise. These tested cases suggest that port density is a useful design variable; they do not establish a scaling law. Actuation. Exact computations modulo $p$ provide access diagnostics; the v14.1 rational certificates establish the repaired claims below. Without interventions, even 60 ports leave under half the branch coordinates with zero modular kernel support. Intervening only at buses that already carry a shunt or a generator gives a modular diagnostic count of of IEEE 118's branches and of IEEE 300's. Its modular diagnostic count is of PEGASE 1354's, where the source data place shunts at most buses. The mathematics is ready. Whether a grid can be measured this way depends on where calibrated switching exists. Strong interventions. A second pair strength selects the true root of the pair quadratic for any nonsingular $K$ (Proposition XIV.6.1). On the indefinite grids the Part VIII minus root fails for up to 158 pairs at strong intervention, and the two-strength rule never fails. The threshold of Proposition XIII.1.1 is no longer needed.

§XIV.1The four obstacles

Part XIII ended with a comparison against conventional practice. Phasor-measurement line-parameter estimation reaches errors of 0.3–0.8%, but it needs sensors at both ends of each line and is demonstrated on subnetworks of about ten branches. Topology detection from smart meters recovers connectivity, not values, on feeders of up to 123 nodes. Part XIII recovers every value of a 70,000-bus model exactly. It does so in simulation, from noiseless readings, with grounding interventions available at every bus, and on indefinite grids only below a conservative intervention threshold. Each of these conditions is an obstacle to use, and each is a question with a computable answer. How much noise can one port tolerate? Can an estimator reach that limit? How many ports turn the limit into something phasor-class sensors can meet? How much does it matter that real grids cannot be intervened on everywhere? The answers follow in that order. The last section removes the intervention threshold entirely.

§XIV.2The noise floor of one port

Every reading is a driving-point or transfer compliance $y=e_p^{\mathsf T}(K+\Theta)^{-1}e_q$ under an intervention $\Theta$. With independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma G_{00}$, the Fisher information about the unknowns $w$ is $F^{\mathsf T}F/(\sigma G_{00})^2$, with $$\frac{\partial y}{\partial w_e}=-(b_e^{\mathsf T}x_p)(b_e^{\mathsf T}x_q),\qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial g_i}=-x_{p,i}x_{q,i},\qquad x_p=(K+\Theta)^{-1}e_p .$$ Measuring the unknowns relative to their true values, the Cramér–Rao standard deviation of branch $e$ is $\sigma k_e$, where $k_e=G_{00}\sqrt{[(F^{\mathsf T}F)^{-1}]_{ee}}$. The number $k_e$ is the noise amplification of the experiment for that branch, and no unbiased estimator can do better. It is computed here from the exact Jacobian of the Part XIII reading set, at the Part XII strengths $\theta$ and $2\theta$ with $\theta=1/\operatorname{median}G_{jj}$.

On every grid tested, the one-port known-support experiment needs reading noise between of the port compliance to resolve the median branch to 1%. At $10^{-3}$, the level of phasor-class sensors, it resolves at most of the branches. Replacing the lean reading set by the full $n(n+1)/2$ protocol lowers the median amplification by a factor of 2.1 on IEEE 118 and 3.3 on IEEE 300, at 16 and 41 times the readings. The limitation is therefore physical, not a property of the reading set. Its source is the locality law of Part X: a branch far from the port moves the port reading through $G_{0v}^2$, and that factor is small.

§XIV.3An estimator that reaches the floor

Part XII estimated in two stages. It applied the closed forms of Theorem VIII.1.2 to the noisy readings and then solved the linear system in least squares. The noise therefore passed through square roots and divisions before reaching a system that ignores its correlations. The estimator here works on the raw readings directly. It is Levenberg–Marquardt in relative parameters, with the model's compliances recomputed from the analytic forward model at every step. It starts from the Part XII estimate, which uses only the data. If that start does not fit the data to the noise level, it restarts from nominal models in which every value is off by an independent 10%, which plays the part of a utility's database, and it keeps the start with the lowest cost. The true values are used only to score the result.

At $\sigma=10^{-6}$ on IEEE 118, the median error falls from $5.3\times10^{-3}$ with the Part XII estimator to here. The exception is IEEE 300 at $\sigma=10^{-6}$. None of the seven starts reached a noise-level fit ($\chi^2$ per reading $6\times10^3$), and the median ratio is 1.24. A few branches remain trapped in a local minimum that the other noise levels avoid. It is reported as found.

§XIV.4How many ports a grid needs

The locality law suggests the remedy: shorten the distance from every branch to the nearest port. Let $P$ be a set of $k$ ports, placed by farthest-point sampling in graph distance from the Part XIII port. At every port pair the experiment reads the baseline transfer compliance, and again after one weak intervention at each bus. That is $(n+1)k(k+1)/2$ readings, still linear in $n$ for fixed $k$. A port is a bus where a test current can be injected and a voltage read: the capability of a phasor measurement unit next to controllable equipment.

On IEEE 118, 20 ports, one per 6 buses, give a median amplification of 5.7. The noise allowed for a 1% median rises from $2\times10^{-5}$ with one port to $1.7\times10^{-3}$. The efficient estimator then reaches a median branch error of at $\sigma=10^{-3}$, with of branches within 1%. On IEEE 300 and PEGASE 1354, the same 20 ports, one per 15 and one per 68 buses, allow about $1.8\times10^{-4}$. The tested cases motivate a port-density study; they do not establish a scaling law. At one port per 6 buses, IEEE 118 reaches phasor-class tolerance. At one per 15 and one per 68 buses, the larger grids fall short by a factor of about ten.

The comparison with conventional practice is now direct. Phasor-measurement line-parameter estimation places two sensors on every line it estimates and reports 0.3–0.8% on subnetworks of about ten branches. Here, 20 ports and one intervention per bus estimate all 173 branches of IEEE 118 to a median of from the same class of noise. The price is one intervention at every bus, which is the subject of the next section. The comparison is also not like for like. The conventional figure comes from field data with real sensor errors; the figure here comes from simulated Gaussian noise on the DC model.

v14.1 interpretation. The 20-port IEEE 118 design in the noise experiment has an exact rank-178/179 Jacobian with a one-dimensional kernel supported on six branches. Its 0.37% median score is computed over all branches and its multiport estimator starts from a nominal model generated by 10% perturbations of the source values. This demonstrates useful nominal-model-conditioned performance, and does not supply a full-rank local-identification certificate for those six branch coordinates. Cramér–Rao values here use analytic derivatives evaluated in binary64 and the stated independent Gaussian noise model.

§XIV.5Actuation is the bottleneck

The reading-map Jacobian measures first-order sensitivity at the declared rational model. Full column rank modulo $p=2^{31}-1$ proves full rank over $\mathbb Q$ and therefore local identifiability there. Deficient modular rank alone proves neither rational rank deficiency nor rational coordinate identifiability. In particular, the former claim that modular kernel-support counts can only undercount was false: $J=[1,p]$ has rational kernel $(-p,1)$, but reduction modulo $p$ incorrectly suggests that its first coordinate is individually identifiable. The modular diagnostics in Table XIV.4 show how strongly access choices change the reading map. Their deficient-rank per-branch counts are diagnostic until independently certified over $\mathbb Q$. The v14.1 exact certificate confirms the IEEE 118 shunt/generator-access count: 20 ports and 13,020 readings have rational rank 127 of 179, nullity 52, and exactly 74 of 173 branch coordinates identifiable to first order. Every reading annihilates the complete exact kernel, and each claimed identifiable coordinate has a rational row-span witness. A different design places six ports at buses 49, 87, 72, 1, 108 and 55 and intervenes at every hidden bus. It has full rational rank 179 from 723 readings, improving the retained seven-port loop's 847 readings. Bus 59 alone does not repair the same harmonic pocket; placement matters. The retained seven-port IEEE 300 design is independently certified at full rational rank 409 from 2,121 readings. Full rank gives local identifiability at the model; deficient-rank coordinate statements refer specifically to its linearization. These are access certificates, not noise-error certificates.

Actuation and port placement are binding design variables. Exact rational certificates now separate what a proposed experiment sees to first order from what finite-field diagnostics merely suggest. The six-port IEEE 118 placement demonstrates why a sensor inside the relevant harmonic pocket can be more useful than another distant sensor. Accuracy still depends on conditioning and calibration. An access-design calculation should therefore first certify rank and then evaluate its noise sensitivity.

§XIV.6Strong interventions without passivity

Proposition XIII.1.1 made the protocol valid on indefinite grids by keeping interventions below $\theta^*$, and $\theta^*G_{00}$ is as small as $2.8\times10^{-6}$. Small interventions mean small signals, which by §XIV.2 is the wrong direction. The root ambiguity can instead be removed with one more reading per pair.

Proposition XIV.6.1 (two pair strengths select the root) proved

Let $K$ be symmetric and nonsingular with $G_{0i}G_{0j}\ne0$. For a pair $(i,j)$ read at strengths $\theta_1\ne\theta_2$, with readings $\delta_1\ne\delta_2$, the true $G_{ij}$ is the only common root of the two pair quadratics of Theorem VIII.1.2. The condition $\delta_1\ne\delta_2$ is decided by the readings themselves.

Proof. The quadratic $Q_\theta(x)=\delta_\theta x^2-2G_{0i}G_{0j}x+\dots$ has root sum $2G_{0i}G_{0j}/\delta_\theta$. The true value $x^\star$ is a root of every $Q_\theta$, so the other root is $s_\theta=2G_{0i}G_{0j}/\delta_\theta-x^\star$. Then $s_{\theta_1}=s_{\theta_2}$ exactly when $\delta_1=\delta_2$. If $s_{\theta_1}=x^\star$, then $x^\star$ is a double root of $Q_{\theta_1}$ and is still the common root. Hence the common root is $x^\star$, and it is unique. No sign or definiteness of $K$ is used. $\square$

On the passive IEEE 118 the minus root never fails, as Theorem VIII.1.2 requires. On the indefinite grids it holds far above the guarantee of Proposition XIII.1.1, to $\theta G_{00}\approx0.1$–$0.5$. It fails at stronger interventions: up to 3 pairs on IEEE 300, 127 on RTE 6515, 26 on PEGASE 9241 and 158 on ACTIVSg 10k, where 10 pairs already fail at $\theta G_{00}=0.83$. The two-strength rule fails nowhere, at any strength, on any grid. The guarantee of Proposition XIII.1.1 turns out to be conservative by three to four orders of magnitude, and with one extra reading per pair it is no longer needed.

§XIV.7Where this leaves the method

The four obstacles of §XIV.1 now have measured answers. On noise: one port is limited by physics. Phasor-class tolerance needed one port per 6 buses on IEEE 118. With that density the efficient estimator matches conventional line-parameter accuracy across a whole grid rather than one subnetwork. The tested larger grids motivate checking comparable density, rather than assuming a universal scaling law. On interventions: they are the binding constraint. The exact rank test says, for any proposed set of switchable buses, which branches can be identified, and it is cheap at the scale of real grids. On strong interventions: the two-strength rule removes the passivity condition at the cost of one reading per pair. What remains open is equally concrete. These are simulated readings on the DC model. Field data were not reachable from this environment, so the real-data test of Part XIII's comparison is still to be done. The ambient alternative, which identifies from the grid's own load fluctuations with no interventions at all, is the natural next question. It is the regime in which the conventional methods of §XIV.1 already operate.
